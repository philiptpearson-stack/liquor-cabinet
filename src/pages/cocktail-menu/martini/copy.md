Ah, the Martini!
Probably the most confusing of history's great Cocktails.
Why did James Bond want it shaken when it has no citrus?
Why do so many people leave out the bitters (wrongly!) when it's clearly a variation on the original Cocktail Formula?
→ Old Fashioned — The Original Cocktail
Is it REALLY just a cold shot of vodka (or gin) in a fancy glass?
Well, here's what you need to know:
The Martini used to be **half** vermouth.
Seriously.
In the 1888 edition of New and Improved Illustrated Bartender's Manual; or, How to Mix Drinks of the Present Style, the famous Harry Johnson made it like this:
And get this: in 1904, Frank Newman printed a DRY Martini that was still half vermouth.** He just used dry vermouth.
By the 1910s, a 2:1 ratio of gin to vermouth was standard.
By WWII, it was 3:1.
And by the 1950s, the dryness of your Martini was a status symbol by itself. A dry Martini was fashionable. An extra dry Martini made it sound like you had even better taste. And a Martini with no vermouth at all?
Well, buddy.
Now you're looking like James Bond his very own self.
By the time Sean Connery gave his classic “shaken, not stirred” line in Goldfinger in 1964, Americans had been making jokes about the Martini's vanishing vermouth for over a decade.
Here's how you make one today:
The Cocktail Menu · Classics
Martini
Makes − 1 + drink
2 ½ oz gin
½ oz dry vermouth
1–2 dashes orange bitters
**Glass:** martini glass · **Garnish:** olives or a lemon twist
**Combine ingredients.** Add the gin, dry vermouth and bitters to a mixing glass.
**Stir.** Fill with ice and stir until the drink is good and cold. Then, stir longer! (Seriously. You need some of that ice to melt. Water softens the alcohol-bite and brings the drink into **balance.** *Dilution is part of the recipe.*)
**Serve up.** Strain into a chilled martini glass.
**Garnish.** Olives on a pick, or a lemon twist.
