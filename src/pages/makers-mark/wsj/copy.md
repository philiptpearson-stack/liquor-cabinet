The WSJ article that changed ***EVERYTHING***
On August 1, 1980, Maker's Mark became a *real* company.
At least, that's what company founder Bill Samuels Sr. said anyway.
Because that was the day *The Wall Street Journal* ran a **front-page feature** on Maker's Mark. And nothing was the same for the company, or the Samuels family, ever again.
It told the story of the smallest distillery in America, doing everything backwards. Making whiskey slow. In small batches. And charging *more* for it, while the rest of the bourbon industry was battling to be cheapest.
Then the phones rang.
Within days, letters and phone calls were pouring into the family's small Loretto, Kentucky distillery from *all over the country*. The distillery had to add more phone lines. And Bill Sr., along with his son, Bill Jr., answered letters every weekend.
For over a year.
Because everybody wanted to know the same thing.
Where can I buy this stuff??
But there was just one problem.
There wasn't enough to go around
Maker's Mark usually ages for six or seven years before it goes in a bottle.
That means every bottle they had available in 1980 went into a barrel back around 1974. When ***nobody*** saw this coming.
So even if every truck in America had been lined up at Maker's Mark's gate on August 2nd, 1980, there wasn't enough whiskey to put on them.
But guess what?
The harder it was to find, ***the more people wanted it.***
Suddenly, every bottle was a trophy.
A solution to the bottleneck:
Within a year, it was clear to Bill Sr. that if he was going to get Maker's Mark into the hands of everyone writing letters, he was going to need some ***help***.
He needed to make more bourbon, yes.
But he also needed more barrels to age it in.
And more warehouses to store those barrels.
***And*** he needed a distribution network to get Maker's Mark out to customers across America.
Enter Hiram Walker & Sons, the spirits mega-company behind Canadian Club and Courvoisier.
Bill Sr. agreed to sell the brand in 1981 — and Maker's Mark got some serious help. Money for barrels. And warehouses. And access to a distribution network that would land their whiskey in stores from Portland, Maine to Portland, Oregon.
Just as fast as the aging process allowed.
(Plus, a big, biiiiig check for the Samuels family. Not bad!)
And to their credit, the new owners wanted Bill Jr. to keep running things the same as Bill Sr. always had.
Same recipe. Same patience. Same red wax.
The door Maker's opened
But *this* might be the most lasting impact of all.
Remember how, before Maker's Mark, bourbon was considered the bottom of the bottom shelf?
Well, after 1980, they'd ***proved*** people would pay more for bourbon if it actually *tasted good.* And the rest of the bourbon industry noticed.
***Fast.***
1984: Blanton's, the first bourbon sold as "single barrel"
1988: Booker's, bottled straight from the barrel at full strength
1992: Jim Beam's Small Batch Bourbon Collection: Booker's, Baker's, Basil Hayden's and Knob Creek
1996: Woodford Reserve
Single barrel. Small batch. Barrel proof.
Fancy bottles.
Distillery tours.
So much of what we think of as "premium bourbon" today started on the trail blazed by Maker's Mark.
By a bottle dipped in red wax.
