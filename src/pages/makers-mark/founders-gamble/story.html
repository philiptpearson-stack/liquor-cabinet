The Founder's Gamble.

Meet Bill Sr!

In 1953, a guy named Bill Samuels Sr. and his wife Margie bought a tiny, run-down distillery near Loretto, Kentucky.

Price tag? $35,000.

Now, Bill wasn't new to distilling. His family had been making whiskey a looong time.

But Prohibition had shut down the T.W. Samuels Distillery. Then World War II shut it down again. And by 1943, Bill had had enough.

He sold the whole thing. Family name and all.

But that was far from the end of the story. Within weeks, Bill was dreaming up his comeback!

Experimenting with new recipes.

And imagining the operation that would become Maker's Mark.

What is a "Wheated" bourbon??

Traditional bourbon is made of corn — plus a little rye for spice.

But Bill wanted something softer. Sweeter. More approachable.

Because bourbon had a reputation problem. By the 1950s, it was widely considered a working man's drink. The bottom shelf. The most el-cheap-o of the cheap.

So Bill ditched the rye and used soft winter wheat as the "small grain."

And made a BIG bet on the idea that bourbon's reputation could change.

So here comes Bill with his new-fangled whiskey and this crazy idea that bourbon could actually be good.

Not "good for a bourbon," good.

Good, good. Period.

And expensive, too.

Five words. Maybe the cheekiest ad in bourbon history.

But think about it!

Other bourbons were bragging about being cheap. Maker's Mark walked in and said, nah. Fuck that. We cost more.

On purpose.

But the magic didn't happen in the ad. It happened at the liquor store!

Let's say a customer walks into the liquor store planning to grab his usual bottle of blended scotch. But, wait. Isn't that Maker's Mark — the one from those crazy ads?

He checks the price. And yeah. It is expensive. For a bourbon.

But compared to the imported bottle of scotch he came in for? Right in the same ballpark. In fact, it's even a little cheaper!

So he buys it. Takes it home. Pours a glass.

And, wait a minute! It's actually... smooth! Even a little sweet.

And for the next 20-some years, that was the whole recipe.

Make it soft. Make it slow. Price it proud. Dip every bottle in red wax.

People who found it loved it.

And they told their friends.

But for almost 30 years, Maker's footprint was almost entirely in Kentucky.

Then, in 1980, everything changed.

Bill Sr.'s son, Bill Jr., had a buddy who now wrote for The Wall Street Journal. The writer came for a visit. And stayed three whole days.

And almost overnight, Maker's Mark became a national sensation.
