Margie Samuels.

One of Whiskey's Most Iconic Women

Meet Margie!

Before she married Bill Samuels Sr. in 1937, Margie Mattingly was a chemist!

(No fooling. She earned a chemistry degree from the University of Louisville. In 1933! There weren't a lot of women chemists walking around in 1933.)

And when she and Bill bought the run-down distillery that would become Maker's Mark, Margie got to work.

That iconic Maker's Mark flavor?

That was Margie.

Bill wanted their distillery to make a softer, "wheated" bourbon. But there was a problem. You can't just make a dozen test bourbons and wait years to taste them.

So Margie baked bread.

Loaf after loaf. Each made with a different grain.

Bill tasted them blind.

And the winner? Soft red winter wheat.

That's right. The recipe for Maker's Mark was picked at the kitchen table!

The name, too?

Margie collected fine English pewter. And every good piece of pewter has a little stamp on it: the maker's mark.

It's how the craftsman signs his work. It says, I made this, and I'm proud of it.

So that's what she named the bourbon.

Maker's Mark.

But most of all, that dripping red wax.

Margie didn't want Maker's Mark to look like all the other bourbons out there. Plain glass. Plain labels.

So she designed her own.

She sculpted the bottle's shape out of papier-mâché. She designed the label. She even drew the lettering.

Then came the finishing touch.

She took over the basement for her wax experiments.

And in May of 1958, Margie melted red sealing wax in her deep fryer and dipped the very first bottle.

(And we just want to say: we love the thought of Margie the chemist, experimenting away in her basement and inventing the identity of freaking Maker's Mark. (!!!))

Every bottle of Maker's Mark is still dipped by hand today.

But Margie didn't stop at the bottle.

That distillery in Loretto was old. Victorian old.

Money was tight, and plenty of folks would have just knocked the old buildings down and started over.

Margie said no.

Every building would be saved. And restored. Faithfully.

And look at the place now.

A National Historic Landmark. The first distillery in America to earn that designation, back in 1980.

Today, hundreds of thousands of people visit every year. Walking the grounds of that old distillery Margie wouldn't let them tear down.

Some folks even say she planted the seed for the whole Kentucky Bourbon Trail.

So next time you see that red wax creeping down the neck of a bottle...

That's Margie's mark.
